\section{Discrete Fourier Transform and Fast Fourier Transform Theory}

\subsection{Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)}

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is a fundamental tool in digital signal processing that maps a finite-length discrete-time signal from the time domain into the frequency domain. Given a discrete-time sequence $x(n)$ of length $N$, the DFT is defined as \cite{proakis_fft}:

\begin{equation}
X(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n) W_N^{nk}, \quad k = 0,1,\dots,N-1
\end{equation}

where the complex exponential term
\begin{equation}
W_N^{nk} = e^{-j\frac{2\pi nk}{N}} = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi nk}{N}\right) - j\sin\left(\frac{2\pi nk}{N}\right)
\end{equation}
is referred to as the \textit{twiddle factor}. In this formulation, $x(n)$ denotes the input sequence in the time domain, while $X(k)$ represents the discrete frequency-domain spectrum.

\subsection{Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)}

Given a discrete-time sequence $x(n)$ of length $N$, the $N$-point Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is defined as
\begin{equation}
X(k) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x(n)\, W_N^{nk}, 
\quad k = 0,1,\dots,N-1,
\label{eq:dft_def_short}
\end{equation}
where the twiddle factor is
\begin{equation}
W_N^{nk} \triangleq e^{-j\frac{2\pi nk}{N}} .
\label{eq:twiddle_def_short}
\end{equation}
Equations\eqref{eq:dft_def_short}--\eqref{eq:twiddle_def_short} describe the frequency-domain representation of a discrete-time signal and form the mathematical basis of FFT algorithms \cite{proakis_fft}.

Direct computation of\eqref{eq:dft_def_short} requires $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations. The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) reduces this cost by recursively decomposing the DFT into smaller transforms using the periodicity and symmetry of $W_N$ \cite{proakis_fft}.

\paragraph{Radix-2 DIT Decomposition.}
In the radix-2 Decimation-in-Time (DIT) FFT, the input sequence is split into even- and odd-indexed samples:
\begin{equation}
x_e(r) = x(2r), \qquad
x_o(r) = x(2r+1),
\label{eq:dit_even_odd}
\end{equation}
leading to
\begin{equation}
X(k) = E(k) + W_N^k O(k),
\label{eq:dit_basic}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
X\!\left(k+\frac{N}{2}\right) = E(k) - W_N^k O(k),
\label{eq:dit_basic_2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
E(k) \triangleq \sum_{r=0}^{\frac{N}{2}-1} x_e(r)\, W_{N/2}^{rk},
\qquad k = 0,1,\dots,\frac{N}{2}-1,
\label{eq:E_def}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
O(k) \triangleq \sum_{r=0}^{\frac{N}{2}-1} x_o(r)\, W_{N/2}^{rk},
\qquad k = 0,1,\dots,\frac{N}{2}-1,
\label{eq:O_def}
\end{equation}

where $E(k)$ and $O(k)$ are $(N/2)$-point DFTs of the even and odd subsequences, respectively \cite{proakis_fft}.

\paragraph{Radix-2 DIF Decomposition.}
In the radix-2 Decimation-in-Frequency (DIF) FFT, the decomposition is applied in the frequency domain. The first butterfly stage computes
\begin{equation}
u(r) = x(r) + x\!\left(r+\frac{N}{2}\right),
\label{eq:dif_sum}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
v(r) = \big[x(r) - x\!\left(r+\frac{N}{2}\right)\big] W_N^{r},
\label{eq:dif_diff}
\end{equation}
which are then processed by two independent $(N/2)$-point DFTs to obtain the even- and odd-indexed frequency components \cite{proakis_fft}.

\paragraph{Computational Significance.}
Both DIT and DIF FFT algorithms reduce the computational complexity of the DFT to
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}(N \log_2 N),
\end{equation}
by organizing the computation into $\log_2 N$ stages of radix-2 butterfly operations, each exploiting the structure of the twiddle factors \cite{proakis_fft}.


\subsection{Convolution Computation Using FFT}

In digital signal processing, the linear convolution between two finite-length
discrete-time sequences $x(n)$ and $h(n)$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
y(n) = (x * h)(n) = \sum_{m=0}^{L_x-1} x(m)\,h(n-m),
\label{eq:time_conv}
\end{equation}
where $L_x$ and $L_h$ denote the lengths of the input sequence $x(n)$ and the impulse
response $h(n)$, respectively.  
The resulting output sequence $y(n)$ has a length of
\[
L_y = L_x + L_h - 1.
\]

A direct evaluation of \eqref{eq:time_conv} requires $\mathcal{O}(L_x L_h)$
multiplications, which becomes computationally expensive for long sequences.

A fundamental property of the Discrete Fourier Transform, known as the
\emph{Convolution Theorem}, states that linear convolution in the time domain
corresponds to pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain.
Specifically, if $x(n)$ and $h(n)$ are zero-padded to a common length
$N \ge L_x + L_h - 1$, then
\begin{equation}
Y(k) = X(k)\,H(k),
\label{eq:freq_mult}
\end{equation}
where
\[
X(k) = \text{FFT}\{x(n)\}, \qquad
H(k) = \text{FFT}\{h(n)\}.
\]

The linear convolution result can then be obtained by applying the inverse FFT:
\begin{equation}
y(n) = \text{IFFT}\!\left\{ X(k)\,H(k) \right\}.
\label{eq:fft_conv}
\end{equation}

In the proposed hardware architecture, the forward FFT is implemented using
the radix-2 DIF algorithm, while the inverse FFT is realized using the radix-2
DIT algorithm.

\paragraph{FFT-Based Convolution Flow.}
Based on \eqref{eq:freq_mult} and \eqref{eq:fft_conv}, linear convolution can be
efficiently implemented using the following steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Zero-pad $x(n)$ and $h(n)$ to a common length $N$.
    \item Compute $X(k) = \text{FFT}\{x(n)\}$ and $H(k) = \text{FFT}\{h(n)\}$.
    \item Perform pointwise complex multiplication: $Y(k) = X(k)H(k)$.
    \item Apply the inverse FFT to obtain $y(n) = \text{IFFT}\{Y(k)\}$.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Computational Complexity Comparison.}
For sequences of length approximately $N$, direct time-domain convolution requires
$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ operations.
In contrast, FFT-based convolution requires:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Two FFT operations: $2\,\mathcal{O}(N\log_2 N)$,
    \item One inverse FFT: $\mathcal{O}(N\log_2 N)$,
    \item One pointwise complex multiplication: $\mathcal{O}(N)$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore, the overall computational complexity is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}(N\log_2 N),
\end{equation}
which is significantly lower than the direct convolution approach for large $N$.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Due to its significantly reduced computational complexity and highly regular
computational structure, FFT-based convolution is well suited for hardware
implementation.
The decomposition into FFT blocks, pointwise complex multipliers, and inverse FFT
enables efficient pipelining, parallelism, and memory reuse.
For these reasons, the proposed design adopts FFT-based convolution as the core
computational strategy.
